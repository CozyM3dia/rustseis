Finite Difference Modeling
Finite Difference Modeling provides an accurate, but
compute intensive method of forward modeling the earth’s
response.

Theory
A highly complex velocity and density ﬁeld is easily built
using the Interactive Velocity Editor. Finite Difference
modeling then divides the ﬁeld into a very ﬁne twodimensional grid. Within each rectangular grid point, velocity
and density values are approximately constant, and a
compressional wave can be accurately propagated through
the grid point. As long as the rectangular grid points remain
sufﬁciently small, rapid lateral velocity and/or density
variations are easily accounted for, and propagation of the
wave ﬁeld across the grid point becomes a simple
mathematical process.
Finite difference modeling offers two modes of modeling:
• Exploding Reﬂector is a poststack, zero-offset form of
modeling.
• Point Source simulates the ﬁring of a shot into an array
of receivers with ﬁnite offsets.
Both modeling modes will produce synthetic data or a series
of time snapshots that can be replayed using Finite
Difference Movie.
Exploding Reﬂector modeling
In Exploding Reﬂector mode, points along reﬂectors
simultaneously begin propagating a pressure wave outward
in all directions beginning at time zero. The amplitude of each
portion of the wave ﬁeld at time zero is a function of the
velocity and density contrasts on either side of the reﬂector.
Along a linear reﬂector, all outward moving waves from each
point along the reﬂector cancel except a wave front that
moves outward away from the linear reﬂector. If the reﬂector
terminates abruptly, there will be no cancellation of waves
moving outward from the end of the reﬂector, and a
diffraction event will be produced. For each time increment,

the wave ﬁeld is stepped through every grid within the model,
and a new wave ﬁeld is produced for the next time sample. As
pressure waves intersect the surface of the model, they are
recorded at that particular time as a seismic event. To model
two-way time, all wave fronts move at half the actual velocity
of the model. This way it takes twice as long for an event to
propagate upward from a reﬂector to the surface, which is
the same time it would have taken for the wave ﬁeld to
propagate downward from the surface, bounce off the
reﬂector, and propagate back up to the surface traveling at
the true velocity of the medium. This assumption works for
reﬂected energy because, in the case of a shot and receiver
being coincident, the wave ﬁeld propagates downward to the
reﬂector and then back upward to the shot-receiver pair
along exactly the same path. Moving upward from the
reﬂector to the shot-receiver pair at half the true velocity is
therefore equivalent to propagating a wave downward and
back upward at the true velocity.
Advantages of Exploding Reﬂector modeling
The Exploding Reﬂector mode produces an unmigrated time
stacked section that would have been created if the seismic
data had been recorded with coincident shot-receiver pairs,
that is everything at zero offset. It provides an excellent look
at how events will be created in the vicinity of localized
velocity variations such as near-surface low-velocity lenses,
karsted topography, buried channels, and how the time
arrival of deeper events will be distorted in the presence of
shallower lateral velocity variations or complex structures
such as thrust belts or overhanging salt domes. When
modeling high-fold lines, Exploding Reﬂector runs orders of
magnitude faster than the shot mode.
Disadvantages of Exploding Reﬂector modeling
No attempt is made to model the effect of separating the shot
and receiver (non-zero offsets). When a velocity ﬁeld has no
lateral variation, reﬂections exhibit a hyperbolic moveout
pattern that is easily corrected with the Dix Equation. Shots
and receivers are easily moved back to zero offset, where their
responses can then be stacked. With non-zero offsets,
receivers near the shot encounter a different velocity ﬁeld
than those at far offsets. Moveout becomes non-hyperbolic.
Further, in the case of dipping reﬂectors, CDP smear
becomes a problem as the actual reﬂection point moves
updip along the reﬂector at larger offsets. These offsetOther Docs

Also, multiples are modeled incorrectly. In the Exploding

velocity, so if an upgoing event were to be reﬂected from the
surface back to the original event, and then back up to the

path as it should have. Thus, peg-leg and interbed multiples
all exhibit twice their correct periodicity. Multiples can only
be properly modeled using the Point Source mode.
Point Source modeling
To accurately model acquisition of a seismic line, you must
use the Point Source mode. A point source is ﬁred into a
receiver array having ﬁnite, non-zero offsets. Waves
propagate downward from the shot and upward, if the shot is
buried, at the true interval velocity of the medium. When the
wave ﬁeld encounters a reﬂector, it propagates back up
through the medium. When the wave ﬁeld hits the receivers,
the wave ﬁeld is recorded at that particular time. Direct
arrivals and multiples are also produced.
During Finite Difference Modeling, input seismic traces are
replaced with synthetic data, producing a synthetic dataset
that has the same geometry and headers as the original
dataset. You may want to create a new line for modeling.
Note: This option only works if a shot is on a receiver location
and the receiver spacing is exactly twice the CDP spacing.
Note: Amplitude variation with offset due to variations in
Poisson’s ratio are not modeled with this acoustic
(compressional wave only) code. Modeling of AVO effect
requires elastic (compressional and shear wave) code, which
increases computer run-times by at least another order of
magnitude.
Advantages of Point Source modeling
Since wave ﬁelds traverse the same path as during seismic
acquisition, offset-dependent effects such a non-hyperbolic
moveout, ray kinking, and CDP smear are accurately
modeled. Also, multiples, refractions and direct arrivals are
modeled properly. The wave ﬁeld is propagating everywhere
at true interval velocity, so peg-leg and inter-bed multiples
have proper periodicity. Rayleigh wave modeling requires

Disadvantages of Point Source modeling
The Point Source mode will typically require several orders
of magnitude more compute time than an Exploding
reﬂector model of the same velocity ﬁeld. Each shot must be
modeled as a single propagation through the entire medium
for the trace length of the shot record. In high-fold shooting,
you will have to model many shots to produce a stacked
section. With the Exploding Reﬂector mode, you propagate
a wave ﬁeld through the medium only one time to produce
the equivalent stacked section.
Also, the Point Source mode generates a large prestack
dataset, while the exploding reﬂector mode generates a
relatively small poststack data volume.

Usage
You can model complex geometry, such as large skips in the
data or topography by using the geometry of an existing
dataset. The shot and receiver locations are taken from the
headers of the input dataset but the data on the traces is
replaced by the modeled data. The shot and receiver locations
are determined by taking the x,y locations from the headers
and projecting them to CDP location using the database; the
database must have geometry deﬁned. If you specify to use
elevation, the header REC_ELEV & SOU_ELEV deﬁnes the
receiver and source elevations.
If you do not have an existing dataset with geometry, create
one. Start by creating a dummy dataset using Null Data File
or Synthetic Trc Generation. Then install geometry into
that dataset using standard ProMAX® geometry methods.
To yield an approximate comparison of run times, an
Exploding Reﬂector model measuring 7000 m by 2000 m
requires 12 minutes to produce three seconds of data with a
peak frequency of 20 Hz when running on an IBM RS/6000
Model 350.
To generate a single shot for that same model requires
approximately 3 CPU minutes. Therefore, a reﬂection survey
containing 160 shots requires 8 hours. Larger reﬂection
surveys can be run overnight or during a weekend.

Parameters
Velocity ﬁeld
Select the interval-velocity-versus-depth ﬁeld from the
parameter ﬁles list. This ﬁeld can be created within the
Interactive Velocity Editor or by converting another type of
ﬁeld such as interval-velocity-versus-time using Velocity
Manipulation. The sampling of this velocity ﬁeld for the ﬁnite
difference grid depends on the peak frequency used in the
modeling.
Note: The velocity ﬁeld is linearly interpolated. If a shot or the
receivers are outside the limits of the deﬁned locations of the
ﬁeld, the velocity ﬁeld is extrapolated using the last points
encountered at the edges of the ﬁeld.
Do you want to input a density ﬁeld?
Select Yes to input a density ﬁeld. One modeling trick is to
generate reﬂectors within a homogeneous velocity ﬁeld by
just using density contrasts. These simple, density-derived
reﬂectors can be migrated accurately with time migration
routines. The density ﬁeld can easily be created by the
Interactive Velocity Editor.
Note: The density ﬁeld is linearly interpolated. If a shot or the
receivers are outside the limits of the deﬁned locations of the
ﬁeld, the density ﬁeld is extrapolated using the last points
encountered at the edges of the ﬁeld.

Grid adjustment factor
Enter the grid adjustment factor to change the grid spacing
and time steps. Run times increase as the inverse cube of the
grid adjustment factor. Cutting the grid adjustment factor in
half increases the runtime eight-fold. Sensible values for this
parameter range between 0.5 and 1.5.
The default value strikes a compromise between computer
runtimes and freedom from numerical noise. As a rule of
thumb, there should be an average of seven grid points per
wavelength, with an absolute minimum being 5 grid points
per wavelength for the lowest velocities in the model. The
wavelength at any point in the model is the local velocity at
that point divided by the peak frequency parameter. The grid
adjustment factor is calculated in a linear fashion. For
example, a grid adjustment factor of 0.8 will reduce the grid
spacing by 0.8 and therefore increase the points per
wavelength by 1.0/0.8=1.25. This selection will also reduce
the time step by a factor of 0.8, thereby increasing the
number of time steps by a factor of 1.25. Using a grid
adjustment factor greater than 1.0 will produce a coarser grid
sampling, which reduces run time and increases numerical
noise. A value less than 1.0, has the opposite effect.

Reﬂecting or absorbing boundary condition at the surface?
Select boundary condition at the surface:
• Reﬂecting boundary is a more realistic state.
• Absorbing boundary eliminates surface multiples and
reduces the amplitude of compressional waves moving
out along the surface as trapped waves. The sides and
bottom of the ﬁeld always have the absorbing boundary
condition in order to avoid spurious edge effects.
If you are placing the source and receivers at elevation, you
should specify absorbing boundary condition at the surface.
The effects of a reﬂecting surface with a varying topography is
not properly modeled.
